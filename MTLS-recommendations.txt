mTLS PRODUCTION RECOMMENDATIONS
===============================

Purpose
-------
Summarize how mutual TLS trust works and provide a production-readiness checklist.
This sample demonstrates the concepts; it is not a production-ready deployment.

1. Understand identity stores and truststores
--------------------------------------------
[ ] Requester identity store: requester private key, its leaf certificate, and
    the intermediate certificates needed to present its certificate chain.
[ ] Responder identity store: responder private key, its leaf certificate, and
    the intermediate certificates needed to present its certificate chain.
[ ] Requester truststore: trusted CA certificate(s) for verifying the responder.
[ ] Responder truststore: trusted CA certificate(s) for verifying the requester.

In this self-signed demo, the requester truststore contains the responder's
certificate, and the responder truststore contains the requester's certificate.
Trust is therefore tied directly to those individual certificates.

With CA-issued certificates, each peer presents its leaf certificate and needed
intermediates. The other peer validates a chain to a configured trust anchor,
normally a root CA. The root generally does not need to be sent in the handshake.
A deliberately trusted intermediate can also be a trust anchor, subject to policy.

Both services can use the same CA or different CAs. Each truststore must trust
the issuer chain of the peer it verifies. Never distribute private keys as trust
material: a truststore needs public certificates, not the peer's private key.

2. Establish a managed PKI and controlled trust boundaries
--------------------------------------------------------
[ ] Use a managed private PKI, enterprise CA, or appropriate managed certificate
    service for internal service identities.
[ ] Protect CA signing keys and restrict who may request each service identity.
    Never distribute CA private keys to application instances.
[ ] Use narrowly scoped trust anchors for the intended service population.
    Avoid trusting unrelated public or enterprise CAs unnecessarily.
[ ] Separate production and non-production trust domains.
[ ] Define ownership for issuance, renewal, trust distribution, and incidents.
[ ] Use approved algorithms, key sizes, and lifetimes. Automate renewal early
    enough to recover from issuance or deployment failures before expiry.

3. Validate certificates AND authorize the authenticated service
---------------------------------------------------------------
[ ] Require a client certificate on every protected responder endpoint.
[ ] Validate certificate signatures, chains, validity dates, key usage, and
    extended key usage according to policy.
[ ] Issue responder certificates for serverAuth and requester certificates for
    clientAuth. Give dual-role services the usages they actually require.
[ ] Put the real service DNS name in the responder certificate's subject
    alternative names (SANs). Keep hostname verification enabled on requesters.
[ ] Define a stable client identity, such as a controlled URI or DNS SAN, and
    map that identity to explicit permissions for APIs and operations.
[ ] Reject a valid but unauthorized client even if its issuer is trusted.

CA trust answers "Was this certificate issued by a trusted authority?"
It does NOT answer "May this service invoke this endpoint?" mTLS authenticates
the connection; application or gateway authorization remains necessary.
Do not use a displayed subject name alone as an authorization policy.

4. Protect private keys and credential configuration
----------------------------------------------------
[ ] Generate private keys in the intended workload or a controlled provisioning
    system; use non-exportable keys/HSMs where required and supported.
[ ] Store keys and passwords in a secret manager or tightly permissioned secret
    volumes. Encrypt stored credentials and restrict access to the workload.
[ ] Do not commit private keys, identity bundles, or passwords to source control.
[ ] Do not log secrets or pass production passwords in command-line arguments,
    where they can appear in process listings or shell history.
[ ] Do not use the demo password "changeit" or distribute both services' private
    keys together in a downloadable production bundle.
[ ] Authenticate and authorize certificate-management actions. Protect any
    management UI/API with HTTPS, network restrictions, CSRF protection where
    applicable, upload limits, and auditable change controls.
[ ] Validate replacement material before activation: usable private key,
    matching certificate, complete chain, allowed identity/usages, validity,
    and correct trust anchors. Loading a PKCS#12 file alone is insufficient.
[ ] Define secure startup provisioning after restarts; do not depend on manual
    uploads for recovery or on keys existing only in a previous process's memory.

5. Rotate leaf certificates under the same trusted CA
-----------------------------------------------------
[ ] Issue a replacement certificate, normally with a fresh private key, before
    the current certificate expires.
[ ] Preserve the authorized service identity and correct hostname SANs.
[ ] Stage and validate the new identity store, then activate it atomically.
[ ] Confirm the replacement chain still reaches an existing trusted anchor.
    If so, peer truststores usually need no change. A changed intermediate may
    still require updating the presented chain or intermediate trust anchors.
[ ] Verify NEW TLS connections use the replacement certificate and key.
[ ] Manage pooled connections and TLS session resumption deliberately. Existing
    connections can retain authentication from an earlier handshake; a reload
    does not automatically reauthenticate them.
[ ] Observe errors and all replicas before retiring the old material. Keep a
    controlled rollback option while the old identity remains safe and valid.

Requester and responder leaf certificates can rotate independently. They do not
need to rotate as a pair when both replacement chains remain trusted.

6. Rotate a CA or migrate to a new trust domain
----------------------------------------------
Use a trust-first, identity-second, cleanup-last sequence:

1) Distribute truststores containing BOTH the old and new approved CA anchors
   to every peer that must verify the changing identities.
2) Verify that every affected instance has applied the expanded trust.
3) Issue and activate identities chaining to the new CA, progressively.
4) Test new handshakes, authorization, and all affected communication paths.
5) After migration and the planned overlap period, remove old CA trust and
   retire old identities. Account for offline instances and retained sessions.

[ ] Track truststore versions and certificate versions across every replica.
[ ] Make updates atomic and preserve last-known-good configuration on failure.
[ ] Plan overlap and rollback without extending trust indefinitely.
[ ] For a compromised CA, use an emergency removal plan rather than a normal
    overlap that continues to trust the compromised issuer.

7. Design reloads for availability
----------------------------------
[ ] Use a production-supported TLS reload mechanism or rolling deployments
    with redundant instances, readiness checks, and connection draining.
[ ] Test rotation during concurrent traffic, including long-lived connections.
[ ] Ensure failed reloads leave the service on a safe, known configuration and
    report the failure clearly.
[ ] Close retired clients, connections, and executors when no longer needed.
[ ] Bound request timeouts, concurrency, and resource consumption.
[ ] Retry only transient failures with bounded backoff. Do not blindly retry
    non-idempotent operations; use idempotency controls where needed.

The demo requester replaces its HttpClient/SSLContext for subsequent requests.
The demo responder stops its HTTPS listener and starts another on the same port.
The application process stays up, but listener replacement can interrupt traffic
and is NOT zero-downtime certificate rotation.

8. Secure the deployment topology
--------------------------------
[ ] Prefer TLS 1.3; allow TLS 1.2 only where needed with approved cipher suites.
    Disable obsolete protocols and never use trust-all or hostname bypasses.
[ ] Define where TLS terminates: application, ingress, gateway, or service mesh.
[ ] If a proxy terminates client mTLS, secure and authenticate the backend hop
    according to the end-to-end trust requirements.
[ ] Never accept caller-supplied client-identity headers as proof of mTLS.
    Any forwarded identity must come only from an authenticated, trusted proxy
    that strips spoofed input and enforces the intended authorization policy.
[ ] Restrict network access and prevent routes that bypass the mTLS enforcement
    point. Keep management endpoints separate from application traffic.
[ ] Externalize service URLs, ports, secret locations, and TLS policy instead
    of relying on this demo's fixed localhost addresses.
[ ] Keep JDK, framework, TLS provider, and other dependencies supported and
    patched. Maintain accurate system clocks for certificate validity checks.

9. Prepare revocation and compromise response
--------------------------------------------
[ ] Decide whether and how CRL/OCSP checks are required. Configure and test the
    actual JVM/provider behavior; default trust-manager initialization does
    not establish a tested revocation policy.
[ ] Define handling of unreachable revocation services, cached responses, and
    the availability/security trade-off. Do not silently bypass required checks.
[ ] Consider short-lived certificates with automated renewal; short lifetimes
    reduce exposure but are not an instant-revocation mechanism.
[ ] Prepare a runbook to replace compromised keys, revoke or deny identities,
    remove compromised CA trust, and terminate affected connections/sessions.
[ ] Do not roll back to a known compromised identity or trust anchor.

10. Monitor, audit, and prove the behavior
-----------------------------------------
[ ] Maintain an inventory of owners, issuers, SANs, serial numbers, fingerprints,
    expiry dates, and deployed credential/trust versions.
[ ] Alert on approaching expiry, failed renewals/reloads, handshake failures,
    authorization denials, and inconsistent rollout state.
[ ] Audit credential changes without logging keys or passwords. Limit exposure
    of detailed TLS errors to operators rather than remote callers.
[ ] Test success with approved identities and failure with missing certificates,
    untrusted issuers, wrong hostnames, expired/not-yet-valid certificates,
    inappropriate usages, incomplete chains, and unauthorized valid clients.
[ ] Test invalid uploads preserve the previous working configuration.
[ ] Test independent leaf rotation, CA overlap, old-trust removal, process
    restarts, pooled/session behavior, and rollback under realistic load.
[ ] For a no-client-certificate test, verify a healthy reachable listener and
    a successful authorized control request. A connection failure alone does
    not prove client authentication was enforced.

Production go-live rule
----------------------
Do not promote this learning demo unchanged. Confirm managed issuance, protected
keys, narrow trust, explicit authorization, automated renewal, safe deployment
and reloads, monitoring, and tested incident/recovery procedures first.
